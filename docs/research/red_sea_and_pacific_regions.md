# DCM characteristics and controls: Red Sea (North/Central/South, Sep-Oct 2011) and NOAA tropical/central Pacific island regions

Scope note: these notes cover region-specific DCM facts and the drivers behind them, to support choosing model covariates (depth, MLD, distance from shore, Region, date, ENSO) and regional structure for a model of chlorophyll fluorescence at depth relative to the surface, from CTD casts at 10-150 m. Every fact in "Cited Findings" was read in this session from the linked source; items marked "background knowledge, not verified this session" appear only under Inferences or Gaps.

**Possibly the same data:** the Red Sea data (Sep-Oct 2011) very likely come from the KAUST Red Sea expedition, Leg 1 (R/V *Aegaeo*, 15 Sep-10 Oct 2011, 206 stations between 17°N and 28°N), which Dreano et al. (2016) analysed — [Dreano et al. 2016, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5178994). If so, that paper's hydrography (the GAIW tongue along the Al-Lith banks at 17-18°N in Sep 2011) describes the user's own South and Central casts directly.

## 1. Red Sea: DCM depth, intensity, north-south gradient, GAIW, eddies, coastal effects (autumn context)

### Takeaway
The Red Sea has a permanent DCM. It deepens and changes character from south to north: it is shallow (about 20 m) and fed from below by nutrient-rich Gulf of Aden water in the south, and deep (about 80 m), near the euphotic depth and light-controlled in the north. Mesoscale eddies and a northward eastern boundary current carrying southern-origin water add strong patchiness in the central Red Sea. So a single "Red Sea" profile shape is not ecologically justified, and the latitude (or North/Central/South) structure should interact with depth.

### Cited Findings
- **The DCM is permanent and follows a south-north gradient.** The DCM is "a permanent feature in the Red Sea" across four cruises from 2012 to 2015. Its intensity varies spatially with alternating highs and lows, and it follows a south-north gradient that the authors tentatively attribute to sinking Gulf of Aden Intermediate Water (GAIW) supplying nutrients to the DCM. DCM chlorophyll was lower than in other studies, possibly because of grazing (from phaeopigment/Chl a ratios). The abstract gives no DCM depths. — [Wafar, Qurban, Nazeer & Manikandan 2021, Arabian Journal of Geosciences 14:211](https://pure.kfupm.edu.sa/en/publications/deep-chlorophyll-maximum-dcm-in-the-red-sea/)
- **DCM depth and chlorophyll along the axis.** Pigment data along the axis (16.75-28°N; Feb-Apr 2015, offshore stations only) put the DCM at about 20 m in the south and about 80 m in the north, deepening from 16.75°N to 27°N. In a cyclonic eddy near 27.5°N the DCM rose to about 50 m. The DCM sits close to the euphotic depth (Zeu), which the authors read as light control. TChla was 0.04-0.60 mg m⁻³ at 0-20 m and 0.28-0.78 mg m⁻³ at the DCM. Surface TChla was highest at the two ends of the transect (south and north), so the central section had the lowest surface values. Integrated TChla (0-1.5 Zeu) was similar across regions: 26.3 (S), 27.4 (C) and 29.7 (N) mg m⁻². — [Kheireddine et al. 2017, Front. Mar. Sci. 4:132](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2017.00132/full)
- **Mixed layers and community structure.** In the same study, MLD reached about 115 m at 27.75°N (late-winter convection in the north) and about 80 m within the eddy. Low-salinity (<37) Gulf of Aden water brings cooler, nutrient-rich water to the south, where diatoms are 20-40% of biomass. In the eddy core diatoms are 30-60%, and between the two they are under 15%. Picophytoplankton dominate the upper layers. Prymnesiophytes and pelagophytes peak at 150-180 m in the central and north-central areas, and high-divinyl-Chl-b *Prochlorococcus* occupies 50-200 m. — [Kheireddine et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2017.00132/full)
- **GAIW intrusion: timing, depth and nutrients.** The three-layer exchange at Bab-el-Mandeb runs June-September (summer monsoon), with the main intrusion near 16°N in Jul-Aug. In the Gulf of Aden the nutrient-rich layer lies at about 120-420 m and is shoaled by at least 50 m by upwelling before it enters. Inside the Red Sea its low-salinity core sits at about 50-65 m. NO₃+NO₂ exceeded 10 µmol L⁻¹ at about 75 m within the GAIW, where salinity is 37-38 against 39 at the surface. — [Dreano et al. 2016, PLOS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5178994)
- **GAIW in September 2011 specifically.** In situ data show the fresher, nutrient-rich stream reaching about 19°N. In September 2011 it followed the Al-Lith banks (17-18°N), and an anticyclonic eddy deflected it above 17°N. Summer blooms typically end in Aug-Sep and rarely extend beyond 19°N. Annual Chl-a differs by an order of magnitude between the oligotrophic north and the mesotrophic south. — [Dreano et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5178994)
- **Central Red Sea eddies and boundary current.** CTD surveys at 22.15-24.1°N found that in November 2013 (autumn) an eastern boundary current filled the upper 150 m and carried warm, low-salinity southern water northward. Low-salinity patches of southern origin had higher chlorophyll and CDOM, and lower N:P ratios indicated that the boundary current affected the central Red Sea at the end of the stratified summer. In April 2014 a cyclonic/anticyclonic eddy pair dominated, and "the cyclonic eddy was responsible for an upward nutrient flux into the euphotic zone." The abstract reports no DCM depths. — [Zarokanellos et al. 2017, JGR Oceans 122:9032-9046](https://darchive.mblwhoilibrary.org/entities/publication/ed29560f-d995-5a64-9536-567d7f52a4b6/full)
- **Winter productivity and wind-driven inflow.** Red Sea surface chlorophyll production is mainly a winter (Oct-Apr) phenomenon. The south depends on wind-driven northward advection of Indian Ocean water. A "northern convection area" in the western northern Red Sea behaves like a typical tropical system, where warmer, more stratified conditions limit nutrient supply. — [Raitsos et al. 2015, GRL](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/83431/1/Raitsos_2015_GRL.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The South is likely to look different in Sep-Oct 2011.** The GAIW tongue (core about 50-65 m, nitrate >10 µM near 75 m) sits right where the user's casts sample at mid-depth, south of about 19°N. It ends in Aug-Sep, so the South in Sep-Oct 2011 is at the tail of the intrusion. Expect a shallower, possibly broader subsurface maximum and higher surface chlorophyll there. Inferred from Dreano et al. 2016 and Kheireddine et al. 2017; the 2015 DCM depths are late winter/spring, not autumn.
- **The North in autumn is fully stratified,** before winter convection; the about 115 m mixed layers in Kheireddine et al. occur in late winter. Autumn DCM depth in the north is expected to be at or deeper than the spring value of about 80 m, but no autumn number was found (inference).
- **Why the Central Red Sea can show very large relative enhancement.** The central section had the lowest surface chlorophyll on the 2015 transect, while integrated stocks were similar across regions (Kheireddine et al. 2017). A ratio or log-ratio of deep to surface values is therefore inflated where the surface denominator is smallest. Eddies and boundary-current filaments of southern water (Zarokanellos et al. 2017) add patchy subsurface peaks. The large Central values may be partly a denominator effect, not only bigger DCMs. Test this by modelling absolute fluorescence at depth with surface fluorescence as an offset or covariate, and by checking Central residuals against salinity (southern-water filaments) or isopycnal heave (eddies).
- **Coastal reef effects.** No study found this session measured how distance from shore changes the Red Sea DCM. The southern Red Sea's wide shallow banks (Farasan/Al-Lith), turbidity, mangroves and seagrass (Dreano et al. 2016) suggest that distance from shore and bottom depth will confound latitude in the South. Shallow-bank casts may simply be cut off before a DCM (inference).
- **Recommended Red Sea structure:** a Region × depth interaction (or a smooth in depth by region), plus a cast-level salinity covariate as a tracer of GAIW and southern water (e.g. minimum salinity or salinity at 50-75 m). Salinity tracks the nutrient source directly and is computable from the CTD (inference).

### Gaps
- No autumn (Sep-Oct) DCM depth or intensity by Red Sea region was found. The available numbers are from Feb-Apr 2015 (Kheireddine et al. 2017) or are undated in the abstract (Wafar et al. 2021).
- Kheireddine et al. BGC-Argo float work on the Red Sea (e.g. a JGR 2018 paper on diel cycles and particle absorption) was not retrieved; seasonal DCM statistics from floats were not verified.
- Triantafyllou, Calbet, Pearman and Acker et al. 2008 were not retrieved this session.
- No quantitative study of the Red Sea DCM against distance from shore or reef was found.

## 2. Pacific island regions: DCM at Station ALOHA, island mass effect, equatorial upwelling, Samoa/Marianas

### Takeaway
Around the Hawaiian Islands and the other oligotrophic subtropical islands, the DCM is deep (about 100-125 m, background knowledge) and light-controlled. It deepens and strengthens in summer. Near islands and atolls, phytoplankton biomass is enhanced at 91% of 35 NOAA-surveyed islands, and the enhancement reaches through the full euphotic zone. It is strongest at the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands atolls and weakest at the equatorial islands (Howland/Baker/Jarvis), where regional upwelling already raises the background. At Jarvis, EUC-driven upwelling is strongly ENSO- and season-dependent.

### Cited Findings
- **Station ALOHA seasonal light control.** The depth of 1% surface PAR moves from about 105 m in winter to 121 m in summer, and isolumes shift by up to 31 m seasonally. The deep chlorophyll maximum layer deepens in summer and holds more chlorophyll a. This coincides with removal of 36 mmol m⁻² of NO₃⁻+NO₂⁻ at 90-200 m, so seasonal light controls the lower euphotic zone. — [Letelier, Karl, Abbott & Bidigare 2004, Limnol. Oceanogr. 49:508-519](https://hero.epa.gov/hero/index.cfm/reference/details/reference_id/1686697)
- **Island mass effect (IME) across NOAA islands.** Of 35 islands and atolls in the NOAA CRED/PIFSC study, 32 (91%) showed a significant IME. Nearshore enhancement reached up to 85.6% over oceanic background (range 0.2-85.6%). Drivers and their share of explained variation were:
  - geomorphic type (atoll > island): 34%
  - bathymetric slope (gentler gives a stronger IME, linked to internal waves): 28%
  - reef area: 26%
  - population or human impact: 12%
  - reef area × type interaction: 7%

  SST, precipitation and current variability were not retained. — [Gove et al. 2016, Nat. Commun. 7:10581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757766)
- **Vertical extent of the IME.** NOAA ship surveys (5-300 m, starting about 2-4 km from shore and running 20-30 km offshore) found depth-integrated chlorophyll a increasing toward shore at 21 of 29 islands (73%). The enhancement "propagates well below surface waters and occurs over the full euphotic depth range." Five sites showed temporal variation, and three showed opposite spatial trends between survey years. — [Gove et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757766)
- **Regional IME contrasts.** The Hawaiian Archipelago held the top nine reef ecosystems by percent increase (29.9-85.6%), and the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands showed the most pronounced IME, mainly at semi-enclosed atolls. The equatorial islands Howland, Baker and Jarvis showed a combined enhancement of only 2.6%, attributed to their position at the western edge of the equatorial cold tongue, where upwelling keeps background chlorophyll high. The Marianas (Saipan/Tinian/Aguijan) and American Samoa (Ofu/Olosega/Ta'u) were included in the analysis but not singled out. — [Gove et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757766)
- **IME mechanisms (review).** Mechanisms include current-island and wind-island interactions, lee cyclonic eddies, isotherm uplift where currents meet bathymetry, tidally generated internal waves, and land-based runoff, groundwater and wastewater. Chlorophyll peaks may lie below the surface and go undetected by satellites, and upward motion can passively lift a DCM into an "apparent bloom" without any real gain in productivity. The vertical-mixing imprint is "confined to a thin region around the islands." — [De Falco, Desbiolles, Bracco & Pasquero 2022, Front. Mar. Sci. 9:894860](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2022.894860/pdf)
- **Jarvis Island upwelling.** The Equatorial Undercurrent (EUC) impinges on the west (upstream) side of Jarvis and drives localized upwelling there. Upwelling peaks in boreal spring, when the thermocline and EUC are shallow and the EUC is strong. EUC cores were about 145-180 m and 0.83-1.06 m s⁻¹ in 2000-2002. On NOAA CRED cruises the coldest 25 m temperature was 23.3°C in 2000 (west-east contrast 2.5°C) and 26.8°C in 2002 (contrast 0.46°C). Semidiurnal internal-tide cold spikes of 1-4°C occur. — [Gove 2005, M.S. thesis, Univ. Hawai'i](https://data.nodc.noaa.gov/coris/library/NOAA/CRCP/project/1221/upwelling_variable_jarvis_island_gove_thesis.pdf)
- **Western equatorial Pacific baseline (model study).** Near the equator at 160°E-180°, the nitracline, chlorophyll maximum and weak new-production maximum sit close to the thermocline at about 100 m in non-El Niño conditions. — [Radenac, Dandonneau & Blanke 2005, Ocean Dynamics 55:34-46](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00000/938/590.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Hawaii and Northwestern Hawaiian Islands.** The 10-150 m cast range brackets the expected DCM (about 100-125 m), so the full peak should usually be captured. Casts ending near 150 m may truncate the lower flank. Summer casts should show a deeper, stronger DCM than winter casts (Letelier et al. 2004), which supports a month or day-of-year term, or a light proxy, for Hawaii.
- **Distance from shore.** It is a well-supported covariate, but its effect should be allowed to vary by Region. It is expected to be strong in the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands and main Hawaiian Islands, weak at Howland/Baker/Jarvis, and possibly direction-dependent at Jarvis, where the west side is the upwelling side. Since the IME runs through the euphotic zone and can uplift the DCM, distance may change both the DCM depth and the surface value, and hence the deep:surface ratio, in either direction. Shoreward uplift of a DCM, while surface chlorophyll also rises, could reduce relative enhancement even though absolute biomass rises (inference from Gove et al. 2016 and De Falco et al. 2022).
- **Covariates suggested by IME drivers.** Gove et al.'s drivers point to island-level predictors that may beat raw distance: atoll vs island, reef area, bathymetric slope or bottom depth at the cast, and populated vs unpopulated. These could enter as island random effects or fixed island traits.
- **Equatorial line islands.** Jarvis, Howland and Baker should be treated as an upwelling regime distinct from the oligotrophic islands: a shallower thermocline, a shallower and weaker DCM relative to the surface, and higher surface chlorophyll. Palmyra and Kingman (about 6°N) sit in the North Equatorial Countercurrent region and probably behave more like oligotrophic islands with a moderate-to-deep DCM. That is an inference only; no Palmyra/Kingman profile data were retrieved.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed DCM depth or intensity values were retrieved for the Marianas or American Samoa specifically. Background knowledge (not verified): both are oligotrophic, with a DCM at about 100-150 m; the Marianas lie in the western North Pacific gyre / North Equatorial Current, and Samoa in the South Pacific near the edge of the South Pacific gyre.
- The often-quoted ALOHA DCM depth of about 100-125 m was not confirmed with a number from a fetched source. Letelier et al. (2004) give only the 1% light depth (105-121 m).
- Messié et al. 2022 (Pacific island impacts) was not located. Search returned the De Falco et al. 2022 review and related SAPPHIRE project pages instead.
- No NOAA PIFSC/CRED technical report giving CTD-derived DCM statistics by region was found.
- Palmyra/Kingman: a Stanford thesis on island-generated flow disturbances and chlorophyll near Palmyra exists ([purl.stanford.edu/qy007ht7512](https://purl.stanford.edu/qy007ht7512)) but was not read.

## 3. ENSO effects on thermocline, nutricline and DCM in these regions; is ENSO relevant to the Red Sea?

### Takeaway
ENSO is a first-order control for the equatorial line islands. La Niña shoals the thermocline and strengthens EUC-driven upwelling at Jarvis, and El Niño weakens it and deepens the central-equatorial nutricline. For the western Pacific warm pool, El Niño shoals the nitracline. For Hawaii, the Marianas and Samoa, no direct CTD-DCM evidence was found. For the Red Sea, ENSO correlates with winter (Oct-Apr) surface chlorophyll through monsoon winds, but no evidence links ENSO to the autumn subsurface DCM. With a single Sep-Oct 2011 snapshot, ENSO cannot be separated from Region in the Red Sea anyway.

### Cited Findings
- **Jarvis.** La Niña enhances upwelling at Jarvis by shoaling the thermocline and EUC. El Niño weakens and occasionally reverses the EUC. The 25°C isotherm at 155°W was about 13 m in March 2000 (La Niña), about 55 m in Feb 2001 and about 97 m in March 2002, matching the strongest to weakest Jarvis upwelling across those years. Western Pacific wind reversals send upwelling-favourable Kelvin waves to Jarvis. — [Gove 2005](https://data.nodc.noaa.gov/coris/library/NOAA/CRCP/project/1221/upwelling_variable_jarvis_island_gove_thesis.pdf)
- **1997 El Niño (model study).**
  - Central and eastern equatorial zone: the nutricline deepened and lateral and vertical nitrate inputs fell. Nitrate along 155°W (4°S-4°N) dropped about 40% from Dec 1996 to May 1997 (observed and modelled), and nitrate was gone along 170°W by May 1997. Surface chlorophyll fell about 50%.
  - Western equatorial basin: the nitracline rose at the El Niño peak, raising chlorophyll at the DCM.
  - These are model results with acknowledged excess thermocline diffusion. — [Radenac et al. 2005](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00000/938/590.pdf)
- **1997-98 EUC and chlorophyll.** The EUC was absent or weak at the peak of the 1997-98 El Niño, when equatorial chlorophyll was low, and reappeared abruptly in Jan/Feb 1998 together with a chlorophyll rise. (Author line garbled in the PDF; NASA/GSFC conference paper, about 2000.) — [Wilson & Adamec, OSTST 2000](https://www.aviso.altimetry.fr/fileadmin/documents/OSTST/2000/wilson.pdf)
- **Red Sea and ENSO.** Annual Red Sea surface chlorophyll anomalies correlate with the Multivariate ENSO Index (r = 0.71, p = 0.003, about 10-year satellite record). The mechanism runs through winter (Oct-Apr) monsoon southeasterlies and Ekman transport pushing Indian Ocean water northward in the south. The northern Red Sea responds differently, with weaker winds during positive MEI. The paper does not analyse September or October separately, and the Indian Ocean Dipole showed no relationship. — [Raitsos et al. 2015, GRL](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/id/eprint/83431/1/Raitsos_2015_GRL.pdf)

### Inferences
- **ENSO in the model.** ENSO is ecologically justified mainly as an interaction with the equatorial-island Region, or more precisely through a cast-level thermocline metric (20°C or 25°C isotherm depth), which carries the ENSO signal directly. A global ENSO main effect pooled across all Pacific regions is poorly justified: the sign of the response differs between the western warm pool (El Niño shoals the nitracline) and the central/eastern equator (El Niño deepens it).
- **ENSO during 2009-2013 (background knowledge, not verified this session):**
  - 2009-10: moderate El Niño (central-Pacific type)
  - 2010-11 and 2011-12: La Niña
  - 2012-13: mostly neutral

  Each region was likely sampled under only one or two ENSO states, so ENSO will be partly aliased with Region × year. Check the design matrix before fitting.
- **Hawaii (background knowledge only):** ENSO effects on the Hawaiian DCM are weak or indirect, with the Pacific Decadal Oscillation and NPGO cited at Station ALOHA. Not verified this session, so ENSO should not be expected to explain much Hawaii variance.
- **Red Sea:** keep ENSO out of the Red Sea part of the model. All casts share one date window and one ENSO state (2011, a La Niña year by background knowledge), so it cannot be estimated there. The documented link is to winter surface chlorophyll, not autumn DCM.

### Gaps
- No observational paper giving DCM depth by ENSO phase for Hawaii, the Marianas or Samoa was found.
- Turk et al. 2001 and Radenac et al. 2001 (observed 1997-98 changes in the western Pacific DCM) are cited by Radenac et al. 2005 but were not read.
- No study of 2009-2013 ENSO effects on CTD-derived DCMs at the NOAA line islands was found.

## 4. MLD vs DCM: is MLD the right covariate, or thermocline/nutricline/isopycnal depth?

### Takeaway
In permanently stratified oligotrophic regimes (northern and central Red Sea in autumn, Hawaii, Marianas, Samoa), the DCM sits well below the MLD. Its depth is set by light (an isolume near the euphotic depth) and by the nitracline, not by the mixed layer. MLD mainly matters when it approaches the nutricline, as in winter convection or in upwelling regimes. Thermocline, nitracline or isopycnal depth, and light, are better-justified covariates for DCM depth and shape than MLD.

### Cited Findings
- **Light control at ALOHA.** The seasonal DCM deepening follows isolume deepening (1% light at 105 m in winter, 121 m in summer). The lower euphotic zone lies in the "nearly permanently stratified upper thermocline." — [Letelier et al. 2004](https://hero.epa.gov/hero/index.cfm/reference/details/reference_id/1686697)
- **Red Sea DCM near the euphotic depth.** The Red Sea DCM is close to Zeu, which the authors interpret as light control. It shoals to about 50 m in a cyclonic eddy where isopycnals are domed (surface water 0.4 kg m⁻³ denser in the eddy). — [Kheireddine et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2017.00132/full)
- **Warm-pool DCM at the thermocline.** In the western equatorial Pacific, the nitracline and chlorophyll maximum lie close to the thermocline at about 100 m. — [Radenac et al. 2005](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00000/938/590.pdf)
- **Analogue: Mediterranean BGC-Argo.** Light (an isolume of 0.3 mol quanta m⁻² d⁻¹) is "a crucial forcing parameter that controls the depth of the SCM." The SCM tracked the 1 µM nitracline in the west (difference under 20 m) but sat about 50 m above it in the east. The MLD almost never reached the nutricline in the east (under 3% of the time) and did so about 20% of the time in the NW, mostly in winter. The authors recommend judging MLD relative to the nitracline, not alone. Steeper nitraclines went with thinner SCMs (about 43 m thick in the NW vs about 72 m in the Levantine). — [Barbieux et al. 2019, Biogeosciences 16:1321-1342](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/16/1321/2019/bg-16-1321-2019.html)
- **Uplift of the DCM by vertical motion.** Upward displacement of isopycnals near islands can passively lift the DCM. — [De Falco et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2022.894860/pdf)

### Inferences
- **MLD is a weak predictor.** In these autumn-stratified Red Sea and oligotrophic Pacific casts, MLD is probably small (tens of metres) and only weakly related to DCM depth. Its main role is setting the depth of the homogeneous surface layer, i.e. the "surface" baseline. It is still worth keeping as the upper bound of the uniform layer.
- **Better covariates for DCM position and enhancement.** A cast-level thermocline or pycnocline depth carries the nutrient-supply and ENSO/eddy signals that MLD lacks. Options include:
  - depth of maximum N²
  - depth of the 20°C or 25°C isotherm in the Pacific
  - depth of an isopycnal (e.g. σθ = 24-25 kg m⁻³ in the Pacific; a Red Sea-appropriate surface would need checking, because Red Sea densities are much higher, about 25-28)
- **Isotherms in the Red Sea.** Upper-layer temperatures are high (often >26-30°C in autumn) and the deep water is about 21.5°C (background knowledge), so the 20°C isotherm does not exist there. Use N²-based or salinity-based metrics instead.
- **Isopycnal or isotherm depth as a modelling tool.** Using the isopycnal or isotherm depth, or "depth relative to thermocline", as the vertical coordinate (or as an offset to depth) can collapse regional profile-shape differences into one curve. This is a recommended model test.
- **Light proxy.** No PAR sensor is mentioned. Day of year plus latitude (surface irradiance), or surface fluorescence as a self-shading proxy, can stand in for the light term.

### Gaps
- No Red Sea- or Hawaii-specific study regressing DCM depth on MLD vs thermocline depth was retrieved.
- Optimal isopycnal choices for the Red Sea were not found.

## 5. Alternative cast-level covariates computable from a CTD cast

### Takeaway
The best-supported cast-level metrics are those that locate the nutricline and pycnocline and track vertical displacement by eddies, upwelling or ENSO: depth of maximum N², the depth of a chosen isotherm or isopycnal, a stratification index (density difference across a depth range), and, for the Red Sea, salinity-based tracers of Gulf of Aden or southern water. Fluorescence-derived descriptors (DCM depth, width and peak:surface ratio) can also serve as diagnostics.

### Cited Findings
- **Thermocline as the controlling variable at Jarvis.** Upwelling at Jarvis is predicted from thermocline depth and EUC strength, using the 25°C isotherm depth as the thermocline metric. — [Gove 2005](https://data.nodc.noaa.gov/coris/library/NOAA/CRCP/project/1221/upwelling_variable_jarvis_island_gove_thesis.pdf)
- **Salinity as a nutrient tracer in the Red Sea.** GAIW is identified by its salinity minimum (37-38 vs 39 at the surface) at about 50-65 m, and it coincides with nitrate >10 µM. — [Dreano et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5178994)
- **Salinity and density mark mesoscale features in the central Red Sea.** Low-salinity southern-origin patches carry higher chlorophyll — [Zarokanellos et al. 2017](https://darchive.mblwhoilibrary.org/entities/publication/ed29560f-d995-5a64-9536-567d7f52a4b6/full). A cyclonic eddy shows denser, saltier, cooler surface water — [Kheireddine et al. 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2017.00132/full)
- **Nitracline steepness and SCM thickness.** Nitracline steepness relates to SCM thickness, and MLD is meaningful relative to nutricline depth. — [Barbieux et al. 2019](https://bg.copernicus.org/articles/16/1321/2019/bg-16-1321-2019.html)

### Inferences
Suggested cast-level metrics, in priority order (all inferences built on the findings above):
1. **z(N²max):** depth of maximum buoyancy frequency, a pycnocline proxy usable in every region, including the Red Sea.
2. **Thermocline proxy:** z20 or z25 (20°C or 25°C isotherm depth) in the Pacific only. z20 is the standard ENSO thermocline proxy in the equatorial Pacific (background knowledge). z25 was used for Jarvis.
3. **Isopycnal depth:** e.g. σθ = 24.5 in the Pacific. It captures heave by eddies, internal waves near islands and ENSO.
4. **Stratification index:** Δσθ (e.g. 150 m minus 10 m), or mean N² over 10-150 m.
5. **Red Sea salinity tracers:** minimum salinity and its depth, or salinity at 50-75 m, as a GAIW or southern-water tracer.
6. **MLD:** keep as a descriptor of the surface reference layer, and possibly as MLD minus z(N²max) to capture MLD relative to the nutricline.
7. **Light proxy:** surface irradiance from latitude and day of year, since a light proxy is needed but no PAR was mentioned.

Model design suggestions (inferences):
- **Vertical coordinate.** Re-express depth relative to z(N²max) or to an isopycnal, and compare its fit with raw depth.
- **Region structure.** Use Region-specific smooths of depth, or random slopes by Region, with island as a random effect nested in Region.
- **Distance from shore.** Let distance interact with Region, and with island type (atoll vs island) where available.
- **ENSO.** Use it only through the thermocline metric or as an interaction with the equatorial Region. Drop it for the Red Sea.
- **Central Red Sea diagnostic.** Model absolute deep fluorescence with surface fluorescence as a covariate, to test whether large Central values reflect a small surface denominator.

Fluorescence caveat (background knowledge, not verified this session): daytime non-photochemical quenching lowers near-surface fluorescence, which can inflate deep:surface ratios in daytime casts. Time of day (or solar elevation) is therefore a candidate nuisance covariate when the response is a relative enhancement.

- The fluorescence-to-chlorophyll calibration and quenching correction applied to the NOAA and KAUST CTD data are unknown.
